What a raise is worth, in hours you no longer have to work

By Jackson Teixeira. Published @@TODAY_LABEL@@. @@READ_TIME@@.

A raise is usually described in dollars: two dollars more an hour, or a few thousand more a year. That undersells it. A raise changes the price of everything you will ever buy, because every price in your life is measured against what one hour of your work pays. When your hour is worth more, the same things cost fewer hours.

Every price gets smaller

Take a raise from $20 to $22 an hour, a 10% raise. Nothing in the store changed price, but look at what happens to the hours each item costs, using the US prices on our What things cost pages:

Nintendo Switch 2, $499.9925 h at $20, 22.7 h at $22
PlayStation 5, $649.9932.5 h at $20, 29.5 h at $22
iPhone 17, $79940 h at $20, 36.3 h at $22
MacBook Air, $1,09955 h at $20, 50 h at $22

A 10% raise cuts the hours of every price by about 9.1%. The rule is simple: divide 1 by the new pay as a multiple of the old one. A raise of 1.1 times means everything costs 1 ÷ 1.1, or 90.9%, of the hours it used to.

What it means over a year

The bigger effect is on your whole life, not one purchase. Say you spend $36,000 a year on everything: rent, food, bills, fun. At $20 an hour, that takes 1,800 hours of work. At $22 an hour, the same life takes 1,636 hours.

The raise freed 164 hours a year. That is about 4.1 weeks of full-time work that your money no longer needs from you. You can spend those hours as extra savings, as a goal that arrives sooner, or, if your job allows it, as time off. The raise did not just add money. It gave you time.

Keep the raise working for you

There is a common trap here. When pay goes up, spending tends to rise to meet it: a nicer apartment, a newer car, more takeout. Within a year, the new pay feels exactly as tight as the old one, and the freed hours are gone. Spending more is not wrong, but it helps to decide on purpose.

A simple plan: before the first bigger paycheck arrives, decide where the difference goes. Some people send half of every raise straight to savings or debt, and enjoy the other half. That way the raise keeps buying back time, instead of quietly raising the cost of living.

How to get there

Raises do not only happen once a year on someone else's schedule. A few things put more of it in your hands:

  • Keep a list of results. Write down problems you solved, money you saved and work you took on. When you ask for a raise, specific results are much easier to say yes to than general effort.
  • Know your market rate. Look at job listings for your role in your area. If they pay more than you earn, that is useful information for your next conversation.
  • Learn one skill that pays. A certification, a tool your field depends on, or a responsibility nobody else wants can move you into a higher pay band.
  • Be open to moving. Changing jobs is often the fastest way to a bigger raise. Even an offer you do not take tells you what your hours are worth.
  • Ask. Many raises happen simply because someone asked, with a clear reason and a specific number.

Use the number

Before your next pay conversation, put a price you care about into the calculator at your current pay and at the pay you are aiming for. Seeing a goal shrink from 40 hours to 36 makes the number you ask for feel real, and worth fighting for.

This guide is general information, not financial advice. Example numbers are illustrative.
