How many hours of work is an emergency fund?

By Jackson Teixeira. Published @@TODAY_LABEL@@. @@READ_TIME@@.

An emergency fund is money set aside for the things you cannot plan: a car repair, a medical bill, a few weeks without a paycheck. The target can look out of reach when you see it in dollars. In hours of work it becomes something you can plan around: so many hours to earn, so many hours a week set aside, and a date when you get there.

How big should it be?

There is no single right amount. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says the amount you need depends on your situation, and suggests looking at the unexpected expenses you have had in the past and what they cost. It also points out that even a small amount gives you some security.

A common way to set a bigger goal is to count a number of months of essential expenses: rent or mortgage, utilities, food, insurance, transport and minimum debt payments. Three months and six months are popular targets. A single income, an irregular paycheck or a job that is hard to replace are reasons to aim higher.

Turn the target into hours

Add up one month of essential expenses, multiply by the number of months you want to cover, and divide by your hourly pay.

Three months of expenses in this example is the pay from nine full work weeks. Nobody saves all of their pay, so the real question is how many of your weekly hours you can set aside. The expenses and wage are examples: put in your own in the calculator.

Pick a pace in hours per week

Decide how many hours of each week's pay go to the fund. It is easier to think "I save 4 hours a week" than "I save 10% of my income", because you can feel what 4 hours is.

  • Saving 4 hours a week at $25 an hour is $100 a week. Reaching $9,000 takes 90 weeks, about a year and nine months.
  • Saving 2 hours a week is $50 a week. The same goal takes 180 weeks, about three and a half years.
  • Saving 8 hours a week, one full workday, is $200 a week. It takes 45 weeks, less than a year.

None of these is the right answer for everyone. The point is to see the trade-off clearly: every extra hour a week you save brings the finish line closer by months.

Start with a smaller goal

A big target can make it hard to begin. A starter goal that covers one common surprise, like a car repair, gives you a win early. A $1,000 starter fund at $25 an hour is 40 hours of work. Saving 4 hours a week, you reach it in 10 weeks. Once it is there, keep going toward the larger target.

Make it automatic if you can. A transfer to a separate savings account on payday means the hours are set aside before you have a chance to spend them. Keeping the fund separate from your everyday account also makes it less tempting to use it for things that are not emergencies.

Using the number

Hours also help you see what the fund protects. A $1,200 car repair at $25 an hour is 48 hours of work. Paid from savings, it costs those 48 hours once. Paid with a credit card at a high interest rate and carried for months, it costs more, as explained in buying on credit spends future hours. An emergency fund is hours you earn ahead of time so that a bad week does not take extra hours from you later.
