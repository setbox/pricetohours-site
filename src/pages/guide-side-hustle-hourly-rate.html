Your side hustle's real hourly rate

By Jackson Teixeira. Published @@TODAY_LABEL@@. @@READ_TIME@@.

A side hustle can be a great way to earn more, test a business idea or pay for a goal faster. But the rate it advertises is rarely the rate you take home. Costs, unpaid time and taxes all come out before the money is really yours. Working out the real hourly rate tells you whether the extra hours are worth it, and how much a purchase costs when you pay for it with side income.

Start with the headline rate

The headline rate is what you earned divided by the hours you were actively working. Say you drive deliveries on weekends, and one month you earned $600 for 30 hours on the road. That looks like $20 an hour. It is a useful start, but it leaves three things out.

Subtract costs, add unpaid time

First, the costs. Gas, wear on your car, the share of your phone plan you use for the work, supplies, platform fees. These are real money that the work used up. Second, the unpaid time: waiting for orders, driving back, answering messages, keeping records. If you would not spend that time without the hustle, it belongs in the hours.

Third, taxes. In the US, if your net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more in a year, you generally owe self-employment tax. The IRS sets the rate at 15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. It applies to 92.35% of your net earnings, and income tax may come on top of it.

Here is the delivery example with all three. The costs are example numbers:

Earned in a month, 30 hours on the road$600.00, or $20.00 an hour
Gas $90, extra car wear $60, phone share $15minus $165.00
Net profit$435.00
Self-employment tax: $435 × 92.35% × 15.3%minus $61.46
Hours, with 6 unpaid hours of waiting and admin36 hours
Real hourly rate: $373.54 ÷ 36$10.38 an hour

The $20 hour is really about $10.38, before any income tax. That does not make the hustle a bad idea. It means you should use the real number, not the headline one, when you decide.

What it does to prices

If you are working extra hours to pay for something, the real rate is the one that matters. A $500 purchase looks like 25 hours at $20 an hour. At the real $10.38, it is 48.2 hours. Knowing that up front helps you plan how many weekends a goal will really take, and avoids the frustration of a goal that keeps moving further away.

How to raise the real rate

The good news is that the real rate is easier to improve than a salary. Every cost you cut and every unpaid hour you remove goes straight into it.

  • Track for one month. Write down every hour and every cost, including the small ones. Most people find one or two surprises.
  • Cut the dead time. Work only the busiest hours, group errands, and say no to jobs that pay little for the time they take.
  • Raise your price. If you set your own rates, as a tutor, designer or handyman, even a small increase lifts the real rate more than you might expect, because your costs stay the same.
  • Keep records for taxes. Business costs can usually be deducted from your self-employment income, which lowers the tax. Keep receipts and a mileage log.
  • Build something that scales. The best side projects stop trading every hour for money. A product, a course or a service you can package earns while you are not working.

Use the number

Once you know your real rate, put it into the calculator instead of the headline rate. Then compare it with your main job. If your side hustle pays less per real hour than overtime or a raise would, that is worth knowing. If it pays more, or teaches you skills that could become a business, you may have found something worth growing.
