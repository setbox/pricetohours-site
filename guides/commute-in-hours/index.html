The hidden hours of your commute

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 4 minute read.

Your paycheck counts the hours you spend at work. It does not count the hours you spend getting there and back, or the money it takes to make the trip. Both come out of your life all the same. Once you add them in, the real hourly rate of a job can be a lot lower than the number in the offer letter.

The time you are not paid for

Start with the clock. If you drive 45 minutes each way, that is 1.5 hours a day spent on work that nobody pays for. Over a five-day week it is 7.5 hours, almost one more workday. Over 250 workdays a year it is 375 hours, which is more than nine full 40-hour weeks.

Those hours are not free time. You cannot cook, sleep, see friends or take a second job while you are on the road. For the question this site asks, how much of your life goes into a purchase, they belong next to the hours you work.

The money it costs to get there

Then add the cost of the trip. For a car, a simple reference is the IRS business standard mileage rate, which is meant to cover the full cost of running a vehicle: fuel, maintenance, insurance and the value the car loses as it ages. The IRS set it at 76 cents a mile for July 1 to December 31, 2026, up from 72.5 cents in the first half of the year. Your own cost may be higher or lower, but it is a fair starting point.

Turn that cost into hours by dividing it by your hourly pay. A 25-mile drive each way is 50 miles a day. At 76 cents a mile that is $38 a day, and at $25 an hour it takes 1.5 hours of work just to pay for the drive.

Pay for an 8-hour day at $25 an hour$200
50 miles of driving at 76 cents a mile−$38
Hours spent: 8 at work plus 1.5 on the road9.5 hours
Real hourly rate: $162 ÷ 9.5 hours$17.05

The job pays $25 an hour on paper. Counting the drive, it pays $17.05 for every hour it takes from your day, about 32% less. The wage, distance and drive time are example numbers: put in your own.

Over a year

Small daily numbers grow quickly. In the example, 250 workdays add up to 375 hours on the road and $9,500 in driving costs. At $25 an hour, that $9,500 takes 380 hours of work to earn. Together that is 755 hours a year spent on the commute, the same as almost 19 full work weeks.

Public transit changes the math but not the idea. A monthly pass is often cheaper than driving, but the trip may take longer. Add the fare to the money side and the ride to the time side, and compare the result the same way.

Comparing two jobs

This is where the real hourly rate helps most. Say a second job pays $22 an hour, $3 less, but it is 10 miles away and takes 20 minutes each way.

  • Driving costs: 20 miles a day at 76 cents is $15.20.
  • Pay for the day: 8 hours at $22 is $176, or $160.80 after the drive.
  • Time: 8 hours at work plus 40 minutes on the road, about 8.7 hours.
  • Real hourly rate: $160.80 ÷ 8.67 hours is $18.55.

The job that pays less per hour pays more per hour of your life: $18.55 against $17.05. It also gives you back about 50 minutes every day. Remote or hybrid days work the same way. Each day at home removes both the drive time and the driving cost from that day.

Using the number

When you look at a job offer, a move or a new apartment, work out the real hourly rate with the commute included. Count the time door to door, not just the time on the highway, and include parking and tolls if you pay them. Then use that real rate in the calculator when you turn prices into hours. A purchase costs more of your life when every hour of pay also carries part of a commute.
