Your discretionary hourly rate: what your time is really worth when you shop

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 3 minute read.

If you earn $20 an hour, a $20 shirt costs one hour of work. That is how most people think about it, and it is wrong in a way that makes every purchase look cheaper than it is.

The problem is that you do not get to spend the whole $20. Taxes come out first. Then rent, groceries, transport and utilities take their share before you decide anything. What is left is the money you actually choose how to spend. Divide that by the hours you worked to earn it and you get your discretionary hourly rate.

A worked example

Joe earns $20 an hour and works 40 hours a week. After taxes, $3,200 a month lands in his account. His essential bills (rent, food, utilities and transport) add up to $1,550 a month. That leaves $1,650 for everything else.

Free money per month$3,200 − $1,550 = $1,650
Free money per week$1,650 × 12 ÷ 52 = $380.77
Free money per hour worked$380.77 ÷ 40 = $9.52
A $20 item costs Joe2.1 hours

Joe's pay is $20 an hour, but his free hourly rate is $9.52. A $20 item costs him a little over two hours of his life, not one. A $5 latte costs about 32 minutes.

We multiply by 12 and divide by 52 to get a weekly figure because months have different numbers of workdays. Using four weeks per month would make the rate look higher than it is.

How to find your own rate

You need three numbers. Look at your last two or three bank statements so you are working from what really happened, not from what you hope happens.

  • Take-home pay per month. What reaches your account after taxes and deductions. If it changes, use an average.
  • Essential bills per month. Rent or mortgage, groceries, utilities, transport to work, insurance, minimum debt payments. Only what you would still pay in a lean month.
  • Hours you work per week. Include unpaid overtime and your commute if you want an honest number. Many people find this lowers their rate a lot.

Then use the calculator with Show the true cost turned on. It does the same math as the example above.

Using it without becoming miserable

The point is not to feel guilty about every coffee. Some things are worth two hours of your life, and you are the only one who can decide which. The rate is a pause button: it gives you a second to ask whether this is one of those things.

A simple habit works for most people. Before any purchase over an amount you choose, convert it to hours. If the number surprises you, wait a day. If you still want it tomorrow, buy it without regret.

What changes when you pay with credit

Money you already have was earned with hours you already worked. Money you borrow has to be earned later, plus interest. Buying on credit spends future hours shows how much more, with the math.

Method and sources

The weekly figure uses 52 weeks a year and 12 months a year, the same method as the calculator's true-cost mode. The idea of measuring purchases in hours of "life energy" comes from the book Your Money or Your Life by Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez. See how we calculate for every formula on the site.
