Buying on credit spends future hours

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 2 minute read.

Paying with money you already have spends hours you already worked. Paying with credit spends hours you have not worked yet, and interest means you will work more of them than the price tag says.

A worked example

Take a $1,000 purchase on a card with a 24% APR. That is a monthly rate of 2%. If you pay it off in 12 equal monthly payments, each payment is $94.56, and you pay $1,134.72 in total. The interest is $134.72.

Paid in cash, at $20 an hour50 hours
Paid over 12 months at 24% APR56.7 hours
Paid over 24 months at 24% APR ($52.87 a month)63.4 hours
Extra work for the 24-month plan13.4 hours

Stretching the same purchase over two years adds more than a full workday and a half of work, just for the interest. The longer the plan and the higher the rate, the bigger the gap.

The math

For a fixed monthly payment, the formula is payment = amount × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r is the monthly rate (the APR divided by 12) and n is the number of months. Multiply the payment by n for the total, then divide the total by your hourly rate for the hours.

Minimum payments on a credit card are worse than the fixed plans above, because the payment shrinks as the balance shrinks, and the debt takes years to clear. If you only pay the minimum, the interest can approach or pass the original price.

What about 0% plans?

Buy now, pay later plans and 0% promotional financing cost no extra hours if you pay every installment on time. The risk is in the fine print: late fees, and promotions where interest for the whole period is charged if the balance is not cleared by the deadline. Read the terms before you count on 0%.

A simple habit

Before you buy on credit, put the total you will pay, not the price, into the calculator. If the hours still feel worth it, go ahead. If they do not, saving first keeps the cost at the price you see.

This guide is general information, not financial advice. Rates and terms vary by lender. Payment figures are rounded to the cent.
