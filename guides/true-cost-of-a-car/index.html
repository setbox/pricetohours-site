The true cost of a car, in hours of work

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 2 minute read.

Most people judge a car by its monthly payment. The payment is only part of what the car costs. Insurance, fuel, maintenance, parking and the value the car loses every year all come on top, and every one of them is paid with hours of work.

Add up the monthly cost

List everything the car costs you in a typical month. For costs that come once a year, such as registration or tires, divide the yearly amount by 12.

Loan or lease payment$500
Insurance$150
Fuel or charging$150
Maintenance and repairs, averaged$60
Parking, tolls and registration, averaged$40
Total per month$900

These are example numbers. Your payment, insurance and driving habits will be different, so put your own figures in.

Turn it into hours

At $25 an hour, $900 a month is 36 hours of work every month, almost a full work week. Over a year it is 432 hours, or about 10.8 weeks of full-time work. The payment alone was 20 hours a month; the rest of the costs nearly doubled it.

The cost you do not see: lost value

A car is usually worth less every year you own it. That loss never shows up as a bill, but it is real money: it is the difference between what you paid and what you get back when you sell. To include it, estimate what the car will be worth when you plan to sell it, subtract that from the price, and divide by the months you will own it. Add the result to the monthly total.

Compare options in hours

Run the same list for each option you are considering: a newer car, an older one, a cheaper model, or no car at all with transit, ride-hailing and the occasional rental. Compare the monthly totals in hours. The question is not which car you can afford, but how many hours of your month each option takes.

If you finance the car, the interest adds hours too. Buying on credit spends future hours shows how to count them.
