The hours test: one question before you buy

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 4 minute read.

Most spending decisions happen in a few seconds. A price shows up, it looks fine, and the money is gone. The hours test slows that moment down just enough to make a real choice. It comes down to one question: would I trade this many hours of my work for this?

Step 1: convert the price

Divide the price by your hourly pay. If you know your take-home pay or the money you have left after bills, use that instead, because it is closer to what you actually get to spend. Gross or net: which pay to use explains the difference.

A $120 jacket at $20 an hour6 hours
A $1,200 laptop at $20 an hour60 hours
60 hours at 40 hours a week1.5 work weeks

Say the hours out loud if it helps. "Six hours" lands differently from "$120". Six hours is most of a workday: a whole morning and part of the afternoon. Use the calculator for your own numbers.

Step 2: compare with something you value

An hour count on its own can still feel abstract. Put it next to something you already care about. What else could those same hours pay for?

  • A share of your rent: if rent is 60 hours a month, a 6-hour purchase is a tenth of it.
  • A goal you are saving for: 6 hours toward a trip or an emergency fund.
  • Time off: 6 hours of pay could cover most of an unpaid day.

The point is not to feel guilty. It is to compare two real options instead of one purchase against nothing. Sometimes the jacket still wins, and that is a good decision because you made it on purpose.

Step 3: wait on the big ones

Pick a threshold in hours above which you always wait before buying. A useful starting point is one workday: if something costs more than 8 hours of your work, sleep on it for at least a day. For something above a full work week, wait longer.

Waiting works because the pull of a purchase often fades once the moment passes. If you still want it a day later, buy it with a clear head. If you forgot about it, you just kept those hours. A threshold in hours also adjusts itself: when your pay goes up, the same 8 hours covers a higher price.

Online stores make waiting harder on purpose, with countdown timers and "only 2 left" messages. Treat that pressure as a sign to slow down, not speed up. A real deal is rarely the only chance you will ever get, and the hours you would trade for it are the same tomorrow.

A quick checklist

Before any purchase above your threshold, run through these questions:

  • How many hours is it? Price divided by the pay you use.
  • How long will I use it? A $1,200 laptop used daily for four years costs a few minutes of work per day. A $120 outfit worn once costs 6 hours for one evening.
  • What will it cost to keep? Subscriptions, fuel, insurance and repairs add hours every month after you buy.
  • Am I paying it now or later? On credit, interest adds hours. See buying on credit spends future hours.
  • Would I work those hours again for it? If the answer is yes, buy it and enjoy it.

Making it a habit

You do not need to run the test on a cup of coffee. Use it where it matters: big purchases, new monthly bills and anything you feel rushed to buy. After a few weeks, the conversion becomes automatic. You will start to see prices as time without trying, and most people find that this alone changes what they buy more than any budget spreadsheet.
