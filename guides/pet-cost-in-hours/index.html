What a dog or cat costs in hours of work

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 4 minute read.

A pet is not a purchase you make once. The adoption fee is the smallest part. Food, vet visits, supplies and the odd emergency keep coming for as long as the animal lives. Counting that cost in hours of work does not make the decision for you, but it shows the real size of the commitment before you bring someone home.

What the ASPCA estimates

The ASPCA publishes yearly cost estimates for pet owners. In its 2021 update, it put the first year of owning a dog at $3,221 and each year after that at $1,391. For a cat, it estimated $1,904 for the first year and $1,149 a year after that.

The first year costs more because it includes one-time expenses such as spaying or neutering, a carrier or crate, a bed and initial supplies. These are averages from 2021. Prices for food and vet care have gone up since then, and a large dog, a breed with health problems or a city with expensive vets can cost much more.

The same numbers in hours

Divide each figure by your hourly pay. At $20 an hour:

Dog, first year: $3,221161 hours
Dog, each year after: $1,39169.6 hours
Cat, first year: $1,90495.2 hours
Cat, each year after: $1,14957.5 hours

At $20 an hour, a dog's first year takes about four full work weeks of pay. After that, a dog costs a little over 1.5 work weeks a year and a cat a little under that. Try your own pay in the calculator.

Per month and over a lifetime

Monthly numbers are easier to fit into a budget. $1,391 a year for a dog is $115.92 a month, or 5.8 hours of work a month at $20 an hour. For a cat, $1,149 a year is $95.75 a month, or 4.8 hours.

Pets also live for many years. As an example, a dog that lives 12 years would cost the first-year amount plus 11 more years at the ongoing amount: $3,221 plus $15,301, or $18,522 in total. At $20 an hour that is 926 hours of work, about 23 full work weeks spread over those 12 years. The real figure will be higher if prices keep rising or if your pet needs more care as it gets older.

Plan for the surprises

Averages hide the bills that do not come every year. An injury, a swallowed sock or a long illness can cost hundreds or thousands of dollars at once. There are two common ways to prepare:

  • A savings buffer. Set aside a few hours of pay each month in a separate account for pet emergencies, the same way you would build an emergency fund.
  • Pet insurance. A monthly premium turns some of the risk into a fixed cost. Count the premium in hours too, and read what the plan does not cover.

Time is part of the cost too. Walks, feeding, cleaning and vet trips take hours every week that never show up on a receipt. A dog that needs two 30-minute walks a day takes 7 hours a week just for walking, almost a full workday. Those hours are usually the best part of having a pet, but they are worth counting when you decide whether your schedule has room.

Either way, knowing the possible cost ahead of time is kinder to you and to the animal than finding out at the clinic.

Using the number

Hours are a way to check whether a pet fits your life now, not a price tag on a companion. If 5 or 6 hours of work a month sounds manageable, along with a buffer for surprises, you are planning well. If the numbers feel tight, a cat instead of a dog, an older animal or fostering for a shelter are ways to share your home with a pet while spending fewer of your hours on it.
