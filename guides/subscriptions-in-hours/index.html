Your subscriptions, counted in hours of work

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 4 minute read.

A subscription is built to feel small. Ten dollars here, fifteen there, charged quietly on the same day every month. Each one is easy to say yes to, and that is exactly why the total is easy to miss. Counting subscriptions in hours of work turns a pile of small charges into one number you can actually feel.

Add them up first

Open your bank and card statements for the last two months and write down every charge that repeats. Streaming, music, cloud storage, the gym, news sites, apps, software, delivery memberships, game passes. Two months catches the ones billed every other month and the ones you forgot about. Then add up the monthly total and multiply by 12.

Here is an example stack of six ordinary subscriptions, at $25 an hour:

Video streaming$17.99 a month
Music$11.99 a month
Cloud storage$2.99 a month
Gym$40.00 a month
News site$15.00 a month
Photo app$9.99 a month
Total: $97.96 a month3.9 hours a month
Over a year: $1,175.5247 hours of work

Forty-seven hours is more than a full 40-hour work week, or almost six 8-hour workdays, every year, just to keep these six things switched on. None of them looked expensive on its own.

Look at each one in hours a year

The monthly price is the number the company wants you to see. The yearly number in hours is the one that tells you what you are trading. In the example, the streaming service is $215.88 a year, or 8.6 hours of work. The gym is $480 a year, or 19.2 hours. The cloud storage is $35.88 a year, only 1.4 hours.

Now ask a simple question for each line: would I work that many hours for this, if I had to do it today? Most people will happily give 1.4 hours a year for storage that keeps their photos safe. Whether 19.2 hours a year is fair for a gym depends on how often you go. If you went eight times last year, each visit cost you 2.4 hours of work.

A ten-minute audit

Make a short table with four columns: the service, the yearly cost, the yearly hours, and how much you used it in the last month. Then sort the list by hours, biggest first, and go down it with three options for each line.

Keep the ones you use every week and would miss right away. Cancel the ones you did not open in the last month. You can always come back, and many services make it easy to restart. Rotate the ones you like but do not need all at once. Watching one streaming service at a time, and switching when you finish a show, keeps the fun and cuts the hours.

Set a reminder to repeat this every six months. Prices go up, and new subscriptions sneak in through free trials that turn into paid plans if you do not cancel in time.

Yearly plans and free trials

If you are sure you will keep something, a yearly plan is often cheaper. An app that costs $9.99 a month, or $99.99 a year, saves you $19.89 by paying once. At $25 an hour, that is 48 minutes of work you get back. The catch is that a yearly plan is also harder to walk away from, so only switch the ones that already passed your audit.

Free trials work the other way. When you start one, put the end date in your calendar the same day. A forgotten trial that becomes a $14.99 monthly charge costs 7.2 hours of work a year at $25 an hour, for something you never meant to buy.

Use the number

The goal is not to cancel everything. Subscriptions you love are a good use of your hours, and knowing their price in time makes them easier to enjoy without guilt. The goal is to stop paying for the ones that quietly stopped being worth it. Put your own monthly total, times 12, into the calculator and see how many hours your list really costs.
