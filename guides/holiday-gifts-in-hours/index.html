Holiday shopping, counted in hours of work

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 4 minute read.

Holiday spending is easy to lose track of. It happens in small pieces over a few busy weeks, on different cards and in different stores, and every piece feels reasonable on its own. Counting it in hours of work turns that blur into one number you can actually picture: how much of your year goes into the season.

How to do it

Add up everything you expect to spend: gifts, wrapping, shipping, travel, food and decorations. Divide the total by your hourly pay. Then divide by the hours you work in a week to see it in weeks.

Holiday budget of $800 at $20 an hour40 hours
At 40 hours a week1 full work week
One $50 gift at the same pay2.5 hours

In this example the season costs a full week of work. That is not a reason to skip it. It is a way to decide on purpose how big that week should be. Use your own numbers in the calculator.

Set the budget in hours first

Most budgets start with a dollar figure that sounds about right. Try starting with hours instead. Ask yourself how much of your working time you want the holidays to take: a few days, a week, more? Then turn that into dollars.

If you decide the season is worth 20 hours of work and you earn $20 an hour, your budget is $400. With 10 people on your list, that is 2 hours of work, or $40, per person on average. Some people will get more and some less, but the total stays inside the hours you chose.

Hours also make trade-offs clearer. A $120 gift at $20 an hour is 6 hours of work, almost a full day. A $30 gift is 1.5 hours. Seeing the gap in time makes it easier to ask whether the bigger gift says that much more.

When the balance comes due

Holiday shopping often goes on a credit card and gets paid off in the new year. If you pay the full balance when the bill arrives, nothing changes. If you carry it, interest adds hours on top of the ones you already counted.

Say you put $600 on a card with a 24% APR and pay it off in six equal monthly payments. At 2% interest a month, each payment is about $107.12, and you pay $642.69 in total. The extra $42.69 is interest.

  • The gifts themselves: $600 at $20 an hour is 30 hours of work.
  • What you actually pay: $642.69 is 32.1 hours.
  • The difference: 2.1 hours of work that buy nothing you can wrap.

The rate and balance here are examples. Check your own card's APR on your statement. The longer you carry a balance, the more hours it costs, which is the subject of buying on credit spends future hours.

Ways to spend fewer hours

  • Make the list before you shop. Write each name with an amount next to it, and add the hours if it helps.
  • Count the extras. Shipping, wrapping and last-minute travel can add several hours that never show up on a gift list.
  • Agree on limits. A family spending cap or a gift exchange where each person buys for one other person can cut the total a lot.
  • Give time instead of things. A home-cooked meal or an afternoon helping someone can mean more than a gift that took you hours to pay for.
  • Pay before the interest starts. If you use a card, plan to pay the full balance on the first bill.

Using the number

When the season is over, add up what you actually spent and turn it into hours. Compare it with the hours you planned. That one number tells you whether next year needs a bigger plan or a smaller list, and it is much easier to remember than a pile of receipts.
