Gross or net: which pay to use when you count prices in hours

By Jackson Teixeira. Published October 30, 2026. 3 minute read.

When you turn a price into hours of work, the answer depends on which hourly number you divide by. There are three candidates, and each one answers a different question.

Three hourly numbers

  • Gross pay is the rate on your contract or pay stub, before anything is taken out. It is the number most people know.
  • Net pay, or take-home pay, is what lands in your account after income tax, payroll taxes and deductions such as health insurance or retirement contributions.
  • Discretionary pay is what is left of your net pay after essential bills. It is the money you actually choose how to spend.

Each step down is smaller, so the same price costs more hours at each step.

An example

Say you earn $25 an hour and work 40 hours a week. That is $4,333.33 a month before taxes. For this example, assume deductions take 22% of it, so $3,380 reaches your account. Your take-home hourly rate is $3,380 × 12 ÷ 52 ÷ 40 = $19.50.

The 22% is only an assumption for the example. Your real deductions depend on where you live, your filing status and what your employer takes out. Your pay stub shows the exact numbers.

Which one to use

Use net pay for everyday decisions. It is the money you can actually spend, so it gives an honest answer to the question "how long did I work for this?" If you only know your gross pay, divide your monthly take-home amount by the hours you work in a month instead.

Use discretionary pay for things you could live without: gadgets, trips, upgrades, subscriptions. Rent and groceries get paid either way, so the hours that really pay for a new phone come from what is left. The discretionary hourly rate guide shows how to work it out, and the calculator's true-cost option does it for you.

Use gross pay when you compare jobs, raises or salaries, because offers are quoted in gross terms. The hourly to salary calculator works in gross pay for that reason.

Count the hours honestly too

The bottom of the division matters as much as the top. If you regularly work unpaid overtime, answer emails at night or commute an hour each way, your real hourly rate is lower than your contract says. Add those hours if you want a number that reflects the time you trade for your pay.
